# p001 close: preferences recover a ranking before they recover a taste, and noisy raters teach more

**Every preference here is SIMULATED.** The raters are code with a known taste. These numbers
size the app's data collection; they are not findings about what people like.

## The pairs (real rollouts, `records/p001-pairs/`)

Four policies ran on all 512 envs from the same seed, under 5 fixed commands (forward 0.15 and
0.25 m/s, turn 0.6 rad/s, an arc, stand still). The walk profile was used (stumble pushes, no
prone spawns or topples), for 8 s.

- **The distilled students are smoother than their teacher.** Their mean action rate and jitter
  are lower on almost every command. For example, at 0.25 m/s forward:

  | Policy | Action rate | Jitter |
  |---|---|---|
  | velstand | 0.054 | 0.050 |
  | student 256-128-64 | 0.044 | 0.041 |
  | student 128-128 | 0.038 | 0.036 |

  A plausible cause, not tested here, is that regressing onto the teacher's actions (MSE DAgger)
  averages out its twitches.
- **The 128-128 student falls more.** The share of episodes with a fall is 0.8 to 2.1% against
  the teacher's 0.2 to 1.0%, which agrees with b002.
- **alpha_walking is the jitteriest** (Pollen's older gait). All four track about equally.

## Who "wins" depends on taste

The pre-registered simulated taste weights falls ×3, smoothness ×1 and tracking ×0.8 over
standardised features. With it, the ranking is student 256-128-64 > student 128-128 > velstand >
alpha_walking. Falls are rare in 8 s episodes, so even at triple weight they move the average
less than the students' steady smoothness advantage. A rater who hates the occasional fall more
than they notice jitter would rank the teacher first. **That is exactly the question simulated
raters cannot answer and real ones can.**

## Sizing (held-out pairs, 10 draws per point)

| N choices | Noisy rater (τ=2): accuracy, Kendall τ (worst draw) | Consistent rater (τ=0.5): accuracy, Kendall τ (worst draw) |
|---|---|---|
| 25 | 0.670, 0.67 (−0.67) | 0.850, 0.73 (0.67) |
| 100 | 0.713, 0.90 (0.67) | 0.866, 0.73 (0.67) |
| 400 | 0.719, 0.93 (0.67) | 0.872, 0.77 (0.67) |
| 800 | 0.721, **1.00 (1.00)** | 0.874, 0.80 (0.67) |
| noise ceiling | 0.719 | 0.874 |

- **Predicting choices is easy; recovering the ranking is not the same thing.** Held-out accuracy
  reaches the noise ceiling by about 100 choices for both raters.
- **Consistent raters taught the model less.** Near-deterministic choices say "A beats B" without
  saying by how much. Jitter, action rate and wobble move together, so many weightings explain
  the same choices. The fitted taste stays far from the true one (cos 0.47 at N=800), and the two
  closest policies keep swapping. Noisy raters, whose choices come out close to 50/50 on near
  ties, reveal the trade-offs: cos 0.71 and a perfect ranking at N=800.
- **The side bias is recovered at N=800** (0.31 ± 0.08 and 0.30 ± 0.16, true 0.30), but not
  reliably at N=200. `shown.order` has to be recorded and randomised.

## Against the pre-registration

- "τ ≥ 0.8 at about 100 choices, moderate noise": **met** for the noisy rater (0.90 on average)
  but not in every draw (worst 0.67). **Not met** for the consistent rater even at 800.
- "Side bias detectable by about 200": **not reliably**; it takes about 800.
- "Students differ from the teacher mainly in falls": **wrong.** They differ mainly in smoothness,
  in their favour, and a little in falls, against them.

## What this means for the app

1. Aim for **several hundred choices** before a preference model is allowed to rank policies,
   and 800 or more before its weights (the "why") mean anything.
2. **Show close pairs and varied conditions.** Near-ties and different commands are where
   choices are informative; an obviously worse policy teaches the model almost nothing.
3. **Randomise the side and record it.** The bias is real at this scale and is only separable if
   `shown.order` is logged.
4. **Offer "tie".** For near-identical pairs it is the honest answer, and it carries information.

## Not claimed

- Simulated raters with a taste I chose.
- Features are only what the sim measures; "looks natural" may need things none of them capture.
- Four policies is a very small set to rank.
- The joint trajectories (`trajectories.npz`) are saved for the app's A/B screen but nobody has
  watched them yet.
