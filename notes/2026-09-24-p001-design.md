# p001 design: learning what people prefer, before any person has been asked (pre-registered 2026-09-24)

## Question

Craig proposed that Duck Studio's users do RLHF for us: watch two policies do the same thing and
choose. Before building that screen, two things need answers. Can a preference model learn a
person's taste from features duckbatch can measure? And how many choices would the app need to
collect? No human preferences exist yet, so this batch answers both with **simulated** raters
whose taste is known, and says so everywhere.

## Method

1. **Pairs (`duckbatch pairs`).**
   - Policies:
     - Pollen's `velstand.onnx` (the teacher);
     - Pollen's `alpha_walking.onnx` (a different, older gait);
     - duckbatch's 256-128-64 and 128-128 students.
   - Each policy runs on all 512 envs with the same reset seed, so env *i* has the same domain
     randomisation for every policy, up to MuJoCo-Warp's small non-determinism.
   - Commands are fixed by pinning the twist and the head and body command ranges and their
     curricula:
     - forward 0.15 m/s;
     - forward 0.25 m/s;
     - turn at 0.6 rad/s;
     - forward 0.15 m/s while turning at 0.4 rad/s;
     - stand still.
   - Walk profile: stumble pushes on, no prone spawns, no topples. 8 s per episode.
   - Features per policy, command and env:
     - planar and yaw tracking error (while upright);
     - fell (0/1) and time down;
     - action rate, mean |aₜ − aₜ₋₁|;
     - jitter, mean |aₜ − 2aₜ₋₁ + aₜ₋₂|;
     - wobble, the RMS of the roll and pitch rates.
   - Joint and root trajectories are saved for a few envs, so the app can show a pair later.
2. **Preference model.** Bradley–Terry over standardised feature differences, plus a side
   term, because people favour a side, and `duck-feedback/0` records `shown.order` for exactly
   this: P(a ≻ b) = σ(w·(φ_a − φ_b) + β·[a shown left]). It is fitted by regularised logistic
   regression. A tie counts as half a win each way.
3. **Simulated raters.** A hidden taste w* that cares most about falls, then smoothness (jitter,
   wobble), then tracking; a temperature (how noisy their choices are); and a side bias β* = 0.3.
   Pairs are drawn at random from the rollouts. The choices are written as `duck-feedback/0`
   `policy_preference` records (share `research`, client `duckbatch simulated rater`), so the
   whole path from reader to fit is exercised.

## Reported, on held-out pairs only

For N = 25, 50, 100, 200, 400 and 800 preferences, averaged over 10 draws:

- accuracy on held-out pairs, against the rater's own noise ceiling;
- Kendall τ between the policy ranking the model implies and the hidden one;
- the cosine between w and w*;
- whether β is recovered.

## Expectation to beat

- Around 100 preferences recover the four-policy ranking (τ ≥ 0.8) at moderate noise, and
  around 400 at high noise.
- The side bias is detectable by N ≈ 200.
- The two students and the teacher differ mainly in falls, and alpha_walking in smoothness and
  tracking.
- If the features cannot separate the policies at all, no amount of preference data will help,
  and the prototype has to say so.

## Not claimed

- These are simulated raters, not people. The numbers size the app's data collection; they say
  nothing about what people actually prefer.
- The features are what the sim can measure. "Looks natural" may depend on things none of them
  capture, which is exactly what real preferences would reveal and simulated ones cannot.
